### Final PCB Design
For the most part, the final design went smoothly- the biggest issues arrived after the DRC and when finally sending the Gerber files to JLC PCB. Mainly, the DRC had a lot of issues regarding clearance violations, items shorting, thermal relief connection to zone incomplete, front/back solder mask aperture bridges items with different nets. Clearance and shorting were both easy fixes; they required some more moving around parts and changing the overall nets. However, the thermal relief and solder mask aperture were a little more difficult to understand and fix. They did end up being simple fixes like editing the number of thermal relief holes and adding extra vias to help connect ground points. Additionally, when uploading to JLC, I learned you have to use mounting holes rather than edge cuts, as when uploading the Gerber file, it then sees that as parts rather than a board with cuts.

# Final Developments
## CAD and E-Bay
All CAD development here is in Fusion360; the goal was to create a rough sketch to have an idea of what the final e-bay should look like and then, from there, create parts individually to print on my A1-Mini.\
<img width="1504" height="941" alt="Overall e-bay 3D model" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/68215c01-59bf-420c-ae04-105ebf7fdcad" />
Keeping the e-bay vertical was the largest issue that I was concerned with; previously, I had used two 9V batteries housed on a vertical component and on the other side had attached two stratologgers (although that flight had failed, it still worked accordingly with parachute deployment). However, TOBI itself, as a board, sits around the threaded rods and hence needs a housing unit within the electronics bay to attach the LiPo batteries to and hold in place. After some research online, I created this version where there are separate 'boards' creating separation between parts. The LiPo batteries have a compartment they are placed into before placing the top components. There are two options here as well for attaching the batteries to the board: either hard, creating a hole inside of the separation disk in between, or later drilling through the PLA. The PCB will then sit on top of that, keeping it close to the top in case of emergency where the user needs quick access to removal and insertion. The bottom section for now is being left empty, but is a great place for an additional payload and experiment. Spacers are then designed to go around the threaded rods to help house and keep the whole unit in place. Two couplers are on the top and bottom to hold the entire device in place and lock in the threaded rods with the e-bay.  
<img width="672" height="852" alt="Closer look at the PCB placement in the e-bay" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e7da0c42-fe43-4a65-b590-c5c1689cfc4d" />
It ended up taking a couple of modifications to get the couplers correct, with them mainly being undersized based on forgetting the assumption that this does not need a second layer of reinforcement like previous electronics bays I have designed. Since this is pure carbon fibre, it is strong enough to withstand any external acting forces without re-enforcing. After the third version of the coupler, I additionally added threaded rod holes (for a fourth version), LiPo and a separating disk for the inner tube, and more specific measurements for the covers of the internal threaded rods.\

### Unit Testing
Although this project has helped me gain knowledge in avionics, embedded systems, PCBs, and an overall understanding of electronics, an additional goal was to further pursue preparation for industry. After researching several positions and keeping track of languages, expected responsibilities, and additional requirements, one recurring responsibility that I realised I had a gap of knowledge in was unit testing. I have previously done unit testing in my co-ops and in other capacities with personal projects; my co-op had used proprietary software, and my own projects were more case tests written natively in the language I was working in. After some research, I came down to two options: Docker and [Unity](https://github.com/ThrowTheSwitch/Unity) (not the engine). After getting sufficiently through using Docker, I realised that Unity was actually native to Platformio and would create easier integration when it came to case testing, and I later switched. However, the biggest issue I will always run into with unit testing is edge cases; these are the most critical since these devices often deal with pyrotechniques, ensuring there isn't a false launch could be detrimental to a flight and cause early parachute deployment (all launches in HPR are manually controlled by a RSO for L1 and L2 launches, more experimental can occur through Tripoli and L3 but for our purposes there can be no false ignition of a motor only early deployment of parachutes and e-matches). To help combat this, I write truth tables in addition to the Unity tests to help demonstrate clearly, based on given information, how the device should perform, then confirm with the Unity code that the device is performing as stated.
